Hydrogen plant by steam methane reforming

A preliminary design of a plant that makes 100,000 Nm³/h of 99.99% pure hydrogen from natural gas, about 78,000 tonnes a year. We took it from reaction kinetics and optimization in Aspen Plus through heat integration, equipment sizing and a full economic evaluation.

How the plant works

The plant is split into five zones. Natural gas is cleaned and pre-reformed, reformed with steam at 900 °C in fired catalyst tubes, then shifted to turn carbon monoxide into more hydrogen. On the way down to 40 °C the gas raises steam at three pressures, and a pressure-swing adsorption unit separates the hydrogen. The PSA tail gas supplies about 94% of the furnace fuel.

Optimization

The operating conditions were chosen to minimize the variable cost per kilogram of hydrogen, with a limit on carbon formation in the catalyst. The optimum is a steam-to-carbon ratio of 2.5, a reformer outlet of 900 °C and 30 bar, and shift inlets of 350 °C and 200 °C. That gives a variable cost of 0.573 USD/kg H₂ with a maximum carbon activity of 0.38, so no carbon forms.

The pinch sits at the cold end of the LP boiler E-502, where the process gas at 134 °C meets LP steam at 124 °C. The design is within 2.4 MW (under 2%) of the minimum heating target, so no extra heat-exchanger matches were worth adding. The plant raises about 155 t/h of steam: 75 t/h feeds the reformer and the other 80 t/h is exported and sold.

Equipment and materials

The reformer furnace and the PSA make up 83% of the installed equipment cost. The PSA cycle was developed in Aspen Adsorption and meets the 99.99 mol% target with 90 ppm of total impurities, at 83.6% hydrogen recovery. The unrecovered hydrogen is burned as furnace fuel.

Every unit that handles hot hydrogen-rich gas above about 204 °C uses 1¼Cr-½Mo steel, selected with the API RP 941 Nelson curves to avoid high-temperature hydrogen attack. The reformer tubes are cast HP-40Nb alloy, chosen for creep strength at wall temperatures up to 997 °C. The hot end of E-500 also sits in the metal-dusting range, so its alloy is flagged for vendor review.

At 1.00 USD/kg the plant passes every feasibility test, but only just: the break-even hydrogen price is about 0.96 USD/kg. The hydrogen price matters most, followed by the natural-gas price and plant capacity. A 20% fall in either the hydrogen price or the capacity, or a 20% rise in the gas price, would make the project unprofitable.

Why the US Gulf Coast: natural gas there costs 3.90 USD/MMBtu against 10.73 in Thailand, and there are existing merchant buyers for hydrogen. Thailand's merchant market, about 30,000 t a year, is smaller than this plant's output.

What would make it attractive: capturing the CO₂ and selling blue hydrogen. At 1.80 USD/kg, 90% flue-gas capture would need about $137M of extra capital but would raise the NPV from $15.7M to about $91M.
